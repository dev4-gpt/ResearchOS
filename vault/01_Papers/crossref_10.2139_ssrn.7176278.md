# Abstract Summary

This paper addresses the growing [[Enterprise Trust Gap]] in generative AI adoption, observing that despite widespread deployment, many organizations fail to see significant financial impact or establish mature governance. The authors hypothesize that this trust deficit is not primarily due to AI inaccuracy, but rather to three preceding organizational conditions: [[Accountability Mismatch]], [[The Human Correction Burden]], and [[The Confidence Framing Effect]]. They argue that addressing these organizational design issues can restore trust without requiring improvements to the underlying AI technology itself.

# Research Questions/Hypotheses

The central hypothesis is that the [[Enterprise Trust Gap]]—the phenomenon where business users abandon, edit, or override accurate generative AI outputs—is primarily driven by three organizational conditions:

1.  **[[Accountability Mismatch]]**: AI reliance declines when users bear full responsibility for outputs but cannot independently verify the machine's work.
2.  **[[The Human Correction Burden]]**: As enterprise AI usage increases, users face a rising responsibility to independently verify, edit, and reconstruct outputs, rather than a corresponding increase in trust.
3.  **[[The Confidence Framing Effect]]**: Non-expert audiences perceive hedging or uncertainty disclosure in AI outputs as incompetence, leading to increased rejection rates, rather than appropriate calibration.

The overarching claim is that where these conditions coexist, trust erodes regardless of output quality, and addressing them through organizational design can recover trust independently of model improvements.

# Core Concepts

*   **[[Enterprise Trust Gap]]**: The disconnect between the rapid adoption of corporate AI and the lack of organizational confidence in its outputs, leading users to abandon, edit, or override even accurate generative AI outputs.
*   **[[Accountability Mismatch]]**: A condition where users are held solely responsible and accountable for AI outputs but lack the means or capacity to independently verify the machine's work.
*   **[[Human Correction Burden]]**: The increasing responsibility placed on users to independently verify, edit, and reconstruct generative AI outputs before acting on them, which escalates with increased AI usage.
*   **[[Confidence Framing Effect]]**: The phenomenon where non-expert audiences misinterpret uncertainty disclosures or hedging in AI outputs as a sign of incompetence, rather than a necessary calibration, leading to higher rejection rates.

# Key Findings/Results

The paper identifies three mechanisms that produce the [[Enterprise Trust Gap]]:

*   **[[Accountability Mismatch]]**: Users reduce their reliance on AI when they are solely responsible for outputs but cannot independently verify the AI's work.
*   **[[Human Correction Burden]]**: Increased enterprise AI usage leads to a significant rise in user responsibility for verifying, editing, and reconstructing outputs, rather than fostering trust.
*   **[[Confidence Framing Effect]]**: Uncertainty disclosures regarding AI output accuracy increase rejection rates among non-expert users, who interpret hedging as incompetence instead of proper calibration.

**Quantitative Benchmarks/Contextual Data**:
*   88% of organizations have deployed AI in at least one business function.
*   Only 39% attribute any EBIT impact to AI at the enterprise level (McKinsey, 2025).
*   Only 21% of enterprises have mature governance models (Deloitte's 2026 State of AI in the Enterprise, surveying 3,235 enterprise leaders across 24 countries).

The paper concludes that the [[Enterprise Trust Gap]] is fundamentally an organizational design problem, stemming from a structural mismatch between how AI systems convey confidence and how enterprises demand defensibility.
