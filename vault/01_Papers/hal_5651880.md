# Efficient and scalable multimodal learning

**Authors**: Mustafa Shukor
**Published**: 2026 | **Citations**: 0 | **Source**: HAL
**URL**: https://theses.hal.science/tel-05651880v1

## Executive Summary & Abstract
Foundation models have enabled striking advances in perception and language, but their training and deployment costs increasingly limit accessibility, reproducibility, and sustainability. This thesis develops a unified perspective on efficiency in multimodal foundation models — spanning parameters, compute, data, and architectural design — and presents methods and principles that make large multimodal systems both more affordable and better understood. We first study efficient post-training and inference for multimodal large language models, showing that competitive performance on image-, video-, and audio- language benchmarks can be achieved in extreme parameter-efficient regimes by freezing more than 99% of the vision and language backbones and training only lightweight connectors (e.g., eP-ALM and DePALM) ; we then reduce inference cost further by identifying redundancy across layers and proposing selective computation skipping at the token and layer levels. Next, we address efficient multimodal pretraining by unifying tasks and modalities within a single sequence-to-sequence framework (UnIVAL), proposing a general multimodal autoregressive objective for large vision encoders (AIMv2), and deriving scaling laws and compute-optimal guidelines for native multimodal architectures and for choosing optimal data mixtures under fixed budgets. We complement these advances with understanding and control: by analyzing internal representations of frozen and finetuned multimodal LLMs, we characterize implicit multimodal alignment, connect misalignment to failure modes such as hallucinations, and propose activation-steering mechanisms that improve controllability without additional training. Finally, we translate these ideas into practical applications by designing compact multimodal agents for embodied settings, including efficient vision-language-action policies (SmolVLA) and embedding-based prediction for low-latency streaming understanding (VL-JEPA). Overall, the thesis provides a coherent toolbox and set of empirical principles for building multimodal foundation models that are compute-aware, data-efficient, and controllable — helping democratize their development and deployment while improving reliability in real-world use.
