## Abstract
This paper addresses a growing challenge in higher education: the shift from take-home essays to in-person writing exams in response to generative AI. While in-person assessments safeguard academic integrity, they also remove the extended time students once had for deep thinking, drafting, and reflection. To bridge this gap, we introduce a scaffolded pre-exam approach in a second-year Microeconomics Theory course. The intervention combines three steps: timed practice writing, AI-generated feedback guided by an instructor rubric, and structured student reflection. Drawing on self-regulated learning theory and the STRIVE framework for AI-integrated assessment, the exercise aimed to develop economic writing skills while teaching critical evaluation of AI suggestions. Survey data revealed that 91% of students found the exercise valuable, though concerns emerged about maintaining authentic voice and the AI's limited grasp of economic reasoning at the time. Building on these insights, we propose the SCAN-X framework (Scaffolded Critical AI-Nested eXams) as a generalizable model for designing scaffolded pathways that link AI-supported pre-exam practice with in-person assessments. The intervention’s success depends on explicit scaffolding of metacognitive reflection and critical AI literacy, offering a model for courses where writing is assessed but not explicitly taught.

## Research Context & Problem Statement
The widespread adoption of generative AI tools has led to a significant pedagogical shift in higher education: transitioning from take-home essays to in-person writing exams to protect academic integrity. 

However, this transition introduces a critical pedagogical deficit:
- It eliminates the extended periods of time students previously utilized for deep thinking, iterative drafting, and cognitive reflection.
- It presents challenges in courses where writing skills are heavily assessed on exams but are not explicitly taught within the curriculum (e.g., intermediate-level STEM and Social Science courses).

To address this gap, the authors developed and evaluated an educational intervention in a second-year [[Microeconomics Theory Course]].

---

## Theoretical Frameworks
The pedagogical design is grounded in two primary theoretical paradigms:
1. **[[Self-Regulated Learning Theory]]**: Used to model and guide student goal-setting, self-monitoring, and reflective evaluations of their draft outputs.
2. **[[STRIVE Framework]]**: A framework for AI-integrated assessment designed to systematically structure how students interact with artificial intelligence during evaluative tasks.

---

## The Intervention Methodology
The intervention was designed as a three-step pre-exam scaffolding process:

```
[Step 1: Timed Practice Writing] 
               │
               ▼
[Step 2: AI-Feedback Guided by Instructor Rubric] 
               │
               ▼
[Step 3: Structured Student Reflection]
```

1. **Timed Practice Writing**: Students complete a practice writing task under timed conditions to simulate exam constraints.
2. **AI-Feedback Generation**: Students subject their writing to AI tools, which generate feedback guided explicitly by an instructor-provided evaluation rubric.
3. **Structured Student Reflection**: Students engage in metacognitive evaluation of the AI's suggestions, determining what feedback to accept, reject, or modify.

---

## Proposed Framework: SCAN-X
Based on the outcomes of this intervention, the authors introduce the **[[SCAN-X Framework]]** (**Scaffolded Critical AI-Nested eXams**).

### Core Principles of SCAN-X:
- **Generalizability**: Designed as a modular, generalizable model for any course linking AI-supported pre-exam practice with in-person assessments.
- **Critical AI Literacy**: Shifts students from passive consumers of AI outputs to critical evaluators.
- **Explicit Scaffolding**: Emphasizes metacognitive reflection and evaluation of AI errors/biases before the high-stakes, in-person assessment takes place.

## Key Qualitative Insights & Stated Limitations

### Student Concerns & Limitations of AI:
- **Authentic Voice**: Students expressed anxiety regarding the risk of losing their authentic writing voice when incorporating AI-generated stylistic suggestions.
- **Domain-Specific Failures**: The AI models utilized exhibited a **limited grasp of economic reasoning** at the time of the study, occasionally providing inaccurate domain-specific advice.
- **Instructional Dependency**: The success of the intervention is highly dependent on the explicit scaffolding of **metacognitive reflection** and **[[Critical AI Literacy]]**; without this guidance, students risk over-relying on incorrect AI assertions.
